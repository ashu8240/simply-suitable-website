Expense ratio explained: what you pay and why it matters

12 Nov 2026 · 5 min read

The expense ratio is the yearly cost of running a mutual fund scheme, shown as a percentage of the scheme’s assets. You never write a cheque for it. It is taken from the scheme’s assets every day, so the NAV you see is already after it.

The short version

  • The expense ratio is a yearly percentage taken from the scheme’s assets, and it is already reflected in the NAV.
  • It covers the cost of running the scheme. In a regular plan it also includes the commission paid to distributors.
  • Cost is one factor among several. A lower cost does not make a scheme better, and a higher cost does not make it worse. What you receive for the cost matters too.

What it pays for

  • Managing the portfolio, including the fund manager’s team.
  • Running the scheme: administration, record-keeping, custody and audit.
  • Marketing and selling the scheme, which includes the commission paid to distributors in regular plans.

SEBI sets upper limits on these costs, and the limits differ by type and size of scheme. The actual figure for each scheme is published by the fund house.

How it affects you

The expense ratio is taken a little each day, so you never see a separate charge. It simply means the scheme’s NAV grows a little more slowly than the value of its holdings. In rupees, on an average ₹1,00,000 of scheme assets, the yearly cost looks like this:

Expense ratioYearly cost on ₹1,00,000
0.50%₹500
1.00%₹1,000
2.00%₹2,000

Why small differences add up

Costs come out every year, so over a long time the effect compounds. Here is a made-up example. You invest ₹10,000 a month for 20 years, a total of ₹24,00,000. In one case your investment grows at 9% a year after costs, and in the other at 8% a year after costs.

Growth after costsValue after 20 years
9% a yearabout ₹63.89 lakh
8% a yearabout ₹56.90 lakh
Differenceabout ₹6.99 lakh

One percentage point a year changes the final value by roughly 12%. This is only arithmetic. A scheme that costs more may or may not earn enough extra to make up for it, and the example says nothing about any real scheme.

How to compare costs sensibly

  • Compare schemes of the same kind. A scheme investing in shares and one investing in bonds cost different amounts to run.
  • Compare the same type of plan. Regular plans and direct plans have different expense ratios.
  • Look at cost together with the objective, the risk level and how the scheme fits your goal. Cost alone cannot tell you whether a scheme suits you.

Where to find it

The expense ratio is shown in the scheme’s monthly factsheet and on the fund house’s website. It can change, so check the current figure from time to time.

Words to know

Expense ratio
The yearly cost of running a scheme, as a percentage of its assets. Also called the total expense ratio.
AUM
Assets under management: the total value of the money in a scheme.
Regular plan
A plan bought through a distributor. Its expense ratio includes the distributor’s commission.
Direct plan
A plan bought directly from the fund house, with no distributor commission in its expense ratio.

Common questions

Is a lower expense ratio always better?

Not automatically. Cost matters, and so do the scheme’s objective, its risk level and how it fits your goal. Compare costs between similar schemes of the same plan type.

Do I pay the expense ratio separately?

No. It is taken from the scheme’s assets, and the NAV you see is already after it.

Why do the direct and regular plans of one scheme have different expense ratios?

The regular plan’s expense ratio includes the cost of paying distributors. The direct plan’s does not.

Can the expense ratio change?

Yes. Fund houses publish the current figure, so check it from time to time.
