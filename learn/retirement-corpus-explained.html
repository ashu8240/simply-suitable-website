Retirement corpus: how to estimate what you might need

11 Nov 2026 · 7 min read

A retirement corpus is the amount you need to have saved on the day you stop working, so that it can cover your spending for the rest of your life. Estimating it takes five steps and a handful of assumptions.

The short version

  • The five steps are: today’s spending, spending on retirement day, how long it must last, the corpus that supports it, and the gap after what you already expect to have.
  • Inflation and the number of years matter more than most people expect, and small changes in an assumption can move the result a lot.
  • The result is an illustration built on assumptions. It is not a forecast or a promise.

Step 1: Start with today’s yearly spending

Add up your yearly household and lifestyle expenses. Then add any extra yearly costs you expect in retirement, such as medical care, travel or support for family. Next, decide how your spending might change after you stop working. Some costs, like commuting, may fall. Others, like healthcare, may rise. People often use a single percentage for this, and it is an assumption you can change.

Step 2: Move that spending to retirement day

Prices rise over time, so the same lifestyle costs more in the future. The simple formula is:

Yearly spending on retirement day = today’s yearly spending × (1 − change after retirement) × (1 + inflation) ^ years to retirement

Step 3: Decide how long the corpus must last

Subtract your retirement age from the age you want the corpus to last until. Running out of money is a bigger worry than leaving some behind, so many people choose a long horizon. You can try different ages and see how the result changes.

Step 4: Work out the corpus

While you withdraw money each year, what remains keeps earning a return. But your spending keeps rising with inflation. What matters is the return after tax and after inflation, often called the real return:

Real return = (1 + return after tax) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1

The corpus is then the value, on retirement day, of all your future yearly withdrawals, plus any amount you want to leave behind. The example below assumes each year’s spending is withdrawn at the start of the year.

Step 5: Subtract what you already expect to have

List what you expect to hold on retirement day: provident fund or pension savings, gratuity and other employer benefits, deposits, investments and maturity proceeds. Use the value you expect on that day, not today’s value. The corpus minus these assets is the gap. If there is one, you can look at it as a one-time amount invested today, or as a monthly amount invested for the years you have left. Some people also raise the monthly amount by a fixed percentage every year.

Why small changes matter

Here is the same example with one assumption changed at a time:

ChangeCorpus neededCompared with base
Base example₹5.06 crore
Inflation 1 point higher (7%, before and after retirement)₹6.81 crore+35%
Inflation 1 point lower (5%)₹3.77 crore−26%
Return on the corpus 1 point lower (7%)₹5.58 crore+10%

One percentage point changes the answer by a quarter or more. That is why an estimate like this is a starting point for thinking, not a number to treat as certain, and why it is worth revisiting as your situation changes.

What this estimate leaves out

  • Income from other sources, such as rent or a pension, unless you subtract it from your spending.
  • Changes in tax rules, and one-time large costs.
  • Returns that vary from year to year. A single average return is a simplification.
  • Your spouse or dependents, unless your spending figure covers the whole household.

Words to know

Retirement corpus
The amount you need on retirement day to support your spending afterwards.
Inflation
The yearly rise in prices, which makes the same lifestyle cost more over time.
Real return
Your return after tax and after allowing for inflation.
Gap
The corpus needed minus the assets you expect to have on retirement day.

Common questions

How many years should the corpus cover?

There is no single answer. Many people choose a long horizon, because running out of money is a bigger worry than leaving some behind. You can test different ages in the calculator.

Why does the corpus look so large?

Because of inflation, a long retirement, and a small real return. Spending keeps rising, and the corpus has to cover many years.

Does this include my spouse?

Only if your spending figure covers the whole household’s spending.

Is the result a recommendation?

No. It is an illustration of arithmetic using the assumptions you enter. If you would like to talk about your own situation, get in touch.
